
**Paper 1 — Towards Safe Mid-Air Drone Interception (Pliska et al., 2024)**  
This paper builds a complete system that lets one drone chase down and physically catch another drone in a hanging net, entirely on its own with no ground station steering it. It uses a LiDAR sensor (a laser-based 3D sensor, not a camera) to spot the target, then uses math to estimate the target's position and speed, predict where it's about to go, and steer itself there. Its main contribution is fixing a flaw in older "aim and chase" math — the old method basically gave up and stopped reacting properly if the drone missed on the first pass, and this paper fixes that so it can miss and try again. They tested it both in simulation and in real flight, successfully catching a moving target in about 2 seconds.

**Paper 2 — TransVisDrone (Sangam, Dave, Sultani, Shah, 2023)**  
This paper only solves one specific piece of the puzzle: spotting another drone using a regular camera and a small onboard computer chip, fast enough to be usable in real time. It doesn't do any chasing or steering — it's purely about the "where is the target" step. Under the hood, it uses a Convolutional Neural Network (CNN) with a "DarkNet" backbone (specifically CSPDarkNet-53) to detect the physical shape of the drone in each frame, combined with a transformer to track its motion over time. Their main achievement is building a detection method that's both more accurate than older methods and fast enough (33 frames per second) to run on cheap, lightweight onboard hardware, which matters a lot if your drone can't carry a heavy, power-hungry computer. It was tested on recorded video and real hardware speed tests, not in an actual live flight chase.

**Paper 3 — An Autonomous Intercept Drone with Image-Based Visual Servo (Yang & Quan, 2020)**  
This paper proposes a completely different steering strategy compared to Paper 1. Instead of calculating the target's exact 3D position and speed and then predicting where to fly, it just looks at the camera image directly and reacts — if the target dot drifts left, steer right; if it gets bigger (closer), keep closing in — similar to keeping something centered in a video game crosshair. This "reactive" approach is simpler and needs less math than Paper 1's "predictive" approach, but it has a weakness: if the target briefly disappears from the camera's view, this method has nothing to fall back on, whereas Paper 1's prediction math can keep estimating where the target probably still is.

**Air-to-Air Visual Detection of Micro-UAVs: An Experimental Evaluation of Deep Learning" (Zheng et al., 2021)** 
This paper doesn't build a new drone-chasing system — it's a benchmark tool. The authors created the **Det-Fly dataset** (13,000+ images of one drone photographing another in flight), then tested 8 different existing detection methods against it. They found a strict trade-off: heavy two-stage models (like Faster R-CNN) are great at spotting tiny, distant drones but are too slow for onboard hardware, while fast one-stage models (like YOLO) work well for edge compute but struggle as the target gets smaller or blends into ground clutter. It's useful less as "something to copy" and more as a fact-check: whatever lightweight detection approach we pick, we must expect its reliability to drop at longer ranges, and we can use their open-source Det-Fly dataset to train our own system.

**Marker-Less Micro Aerial Vehicle Detection and Localization Using Convolutional Neural Networks" (Vrba & Saska, 2020)** 
This paper tackles a problem Paper 2 (TransVisDrone) didn't touch — not just spotting the target drone in the camera image, but also figuring out how far away it is, using nothing but a regular camera (no LiDAR, no stereo pair). Their trick is simple: the smaller the drone looks in the image, the farther away it probably is, and they built an AI system around that idea. The key finding is a genuine trade-off worth remembering — their camera-only method can spot targets much farther away (beyond 30 meters) than a proper stereo depth camera (which maxes out around 20 meters), but its distance _estimate_ is less accurate than what stereo gives you. This is directly useful for your project if you're deciding between a single camera versus a stereo camera pair, since it lays out exactly what you'd gain and lose with each choice.

**Brain over Brawn Using a StereoCamera to Detect, Track, and Intercepta Faster UAV by Reconstructing theIntruder’s Trajectory** 
This paper builds a full detect-and-catch pipeline using a stereo camera (two lenses for depth perception, like human eyes) instead of LiDAR or a single camera. What makes it interesting is that their interceptor drone is actually _slower_ than the target it's chasing — normally that should be impossible to catch. Their trick is to not chase directly, but instead watch the target fly its repeating figure-eight loop, mathematically work out the shape and position of that loop, and then fly straight to a spot on that path so the target essentially flies into them — like a goalkeeper predicting the ball's path instead of running after it. It worked well in tests (9 out of 12 real-world catches), but the catch is that this only works because the target's flight pattern is predictable and repeating — a real evasive rogue drone wouldn't cooperate like that, so this specific trick likely wouldn't transfer directly to your use case.

---

One thing worth noticing across all three: **no single paper does everything.** Paper 1 handles predicting + steering + catching but borrows a simple detection method. Paper 2 only does detection. Paper 3 does detection + steering but skips the "predict where it'll be" math entirely. This is exactly the pattern I mentioned earlier — you'll likely end up mixing ideas from multiple papers rather than adopting one whole paper as-is.
