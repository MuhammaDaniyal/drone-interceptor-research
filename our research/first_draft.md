Perception: Object Detection & TrackingRunning heavy AI models on every single video frame will instantly bottleneck your system. You must split perception into two parts: Detection and Tracking.Detection Model (Find it): Use YOLOv8 Nano (YOLOv8n) or YOLOv10n. They are explicitly designed for edge hardware.Optimization: You cannot run raw PyTorch models. You must export the model to ONNX, quantize the weights to 8-bit integers (INT8), and compile it using TensorRT to leverage the specific GPU architecture of your board. This pushes frame rates from ~10 FPS up to 60+ FPS.Tracking Algorithm (Keep it): YOLO is computationally expensive. Run YOLO once every 5 frames to find the target, then hand the bounding box to a lightweight C++ tracker like KCF (Kernelized Correlation Filter) or ByteTrack. These algorithms track pixel shifts almost instantly, filling in the gaps between YOLO inferences.3. Spatial: Depth EstimationCalculating distance is critical for knowing when to trigger the final interception.Stereo Vision (Two Cameras): Calculates depth via disparity mapping. Highly accurate at close range, but computationally heavy and fails at long distances (which is where intercepts begin).Neural Monocular Depth (e.g., MiDaS): AI models that guess depth from a single image. Do not use this. It is too slow and will crush your frame rate.Apparent Size Heuristic (The SOTA Approach): The fastest, most computationally efficient method. Using basic pinhole camera geometry, if you know the approximate physical wingspan of the enemy drone ($W_{real}$), the focal length of your lens ($f$), and the pixel width of the bounding box ($W_{pixel}$), you calculate distance ($D$) instantly with zero AI overhead:$$D = \frac{W_{real} \cdot f}{W_{pixel}}$$4. State Estimation: Handling Blind SpotsIf the target flies behind a tree, or the camera drops a frame, the bounding box disappears. Without state estimation, your interceptor will stop in mid-air.Extended Kalman Filter (EKF): You must pipe the bounding box coordinates into an EKF. The EKF maintains a mathematical physics model of the enemy drone's state $[x, y, z, \dot{x}, \dot{y}, \dot{z}]$. When the camera goes blind, the EKF takes over and feeds predicted coordinates to the flight controller so your drone keeps flying toward where the enemy should be until the camera sees it again.5. Guidance: The Intercept AlgorithmDo not program the drone to fly directly at the bounding box. That is called "Pure Pursuit" (chasing the tail), and you will never catch a maneuvering target.Proportional Navigation (PN): This is the gold-standard algorithm used in military missiles. It dictates that the interceptor must maneuver at a rate proportional to the rotation rate of the Line-Of-Sight (LOS).The Logic: If the bounding box of the enemy drone gets larger but stays in the exact same spot on your camera feed, you are on a perfect collision course.The Math: The commanded lateral acceleration ($a$) is calculated as:$$a = N \cdot V_{c} \cdot \dot{\lambda}$$Where $N$ is a tuning constant (usually 3 to 5), $V_{c}$ is the closing velocity, and $\dot{\lambda}$ is the angular rate of the bounding box moving across your screen. You translate this acceleration vector into a setpoint and send it to the firmware team's flight controller.6. Target Allocation: Choosing the EnemyIf your camera sees a swarm of 5 drones, which one does it hit?The Hungarian Algorithm: Your C++ pipeline logs the bounding box of every drone. It assigns a cost matrix to each target based on distance and velocity. The algorithm mathematically selects the optimal target that requires the least amount of battery and maneuvering to hit, locking out the other 4.Deconfliction: If you deploy two interceptors, they broadcast their locked target's estimated GPS coordinate over telemetry. If Interceptor A broadcasts a lock on Target 1, Interceptor B automatically adds a massive "cost penalty" to Target 1 in its Hungarian matrix, forcing it to instantly switch to Target 2.
